Same story, different newsroom

The fastest way to actually learn politics is to read how outlets with different allegiances cover the identical event. None of these is "the truth" and none is "just lies" — each reflects who owns the outlet, who its audience is, and what that audience already believes. Here is how five outlets covering the Middle East differ. Read them side by side daily.

On Gaza / Israel

Al Jazeera

Qatar state-funded · pan-Arab audience. Uses language like "genocidal war" and "Israeli aggression" as standard description, not just in opinion pieces. Centres Palestinian casualties and displacement in nearly every headline.

Haaretz

Israeli · left-leaning · Tel Aviv. An Israeli paper that is nonetheless one of the most consistently critical-of-the-government outlets you'll find anywhere, including in Arab media. Runs investigations on settler violence and Gaza destruction using satellite evidence — while still writing from inside Israeli society, for an Israeli/Jewish-diaspora readership. Being Israeli and being pro-government are not the same thing — Haaretz is proof.

Times of Israel / Israel Hayom

Israeli mainstream-right; Israel Hayom is privately funded and widely seen as close to the government. Centres hostages, military operational updates, and regional threats to Israel; treats "genocide" framing as something to push back on rather than accept; gives far more space to Israeli domestic politics than to Palestinian civilian impact.

Al Mayadeen

Beirut-based · widely reported to be Iran/Hezbollah-aligned (it denies this). Part of the "Axis of Resistance" media ecosystem. Runs a front-page Gaza death-toll counter; frames Hezbollah and Iran as defenders rather than aggressors; had two of its own journalists killed by an Israeli strike this year, which it covers as a press-freedom story.

New York Times

US paper of record. A useful case study in itself: it has been accused of pro-Israel bias (softened headlines, downplayed casualties) and anti-Israel bias (by pro-Israel critics) simultaneously. When the same outlet is attacked from both directions at once, that's usually a sign it sits nearer the centre than either critic admits — though that's not proof of accuracy on any single story.

How to use this: pick one ongoing story. Read it in Al Jazeera, then in Times of Israel, then in a wire like Reuters. Notice what each leads with, what each leaves out, and which words each treats as neutral fact. That gap between them is the actual lesson.

The 2026 Iran War

Assume zero prior knowledge. Every term in bold is defined in the Glossary tab.

This war sits on top of decades of tension over Iran's nuclear program. Since the 1979 revolution that toppled the US-backed Shah (king) and installed a Shia Islamist government under Ayatollah Khomeini, Iran and the United States have been adversaries.

Iran built a network of allied armed groups across the region often called the Axis of Resistance — Hezbollah in Lebanon, the Houthis in Yemen, Hamas in Gaza, and various Iraqi militias. These are proxies: groups Iran funds and arms so it can exert pressure on rivals without fighting directly itself.

Years of failed diplomacy over Iran's uranium enrichment (which Iran says is peaceful energy, and which Israel and the US say is a weapons program) set the stage for Israel and the US to launch direct strikes on February 28, 2026, killing Iran's Supreme Leader.

Why the whole world feels this war: the Strait of Hormuz is a narrow sea passage through which roughly a fifth of the world's oil shipments pass. When Iran closes or threatens it, oil prices spike globally — which is why this conflict shows up in business/markets coverage (Financial Times style) just as much as in human-rights coverage (Al Jazeera style).

Where it stands now: a ceasefire was reached in April 2026 but repeatedly collapsed. The US has used a stop-start pattern of strikes — hit, pause, let diplomacy try, repeat — while keeping a target list as leverage. Iran went through a leadership transition mid-war after its Supreme Leader was killed.

Israel–Gaza / Israel–Palestine

Assume zero prior knowledge.

The core dispute traces to competing claims over the same land after the British Mandate (British colonial rule over the area) ended in 1948, when the state of Israel was created and roughly 700,000 Palestinians were displaced in what Palestinians call the Nakba ("catastrophe" in Arabic).

Wars in 1948, 1967, and 1973 shaped today's borders. Israel has militarily occupied the West Bank and Gaza since 1967 (occupation = controlling territory it does not consider part of its own recognised state). Gaza has been under a tight Israeli–Egyptian blockade since Hamas — an Islamist group designated a terrorist organisation by Israel, the US, and the EU — took control there in 2007.

The current war began after Hamas's October 7, 2023 attack on Israel, which killed about 1,200 people and took roughly 250 hostages. Israel's military response has killed a very large number of Palestinians in Gaza and caused a severe humanitarian crisis.

The genocide question: some genocide scholars and human-rights groups argue the campaign meets the legal definition of genocide; Israel and its allies reject that and describe the campaign as self-defence against Hamas. As the Perspectives tab shows, both descriptions circulate as settled fact in different parts of the media. Treat that as a reason to read across sources — not a reason to pick a side and stop reading.

Where it stands now: a US-brokered ceasefire has technically held since October 2025, built around a "Yellow Line" marking how far Israeli forces would withdraw — but strikes have continued and the line has reportedly shifted in practice, so the truce is fragile.

Western Sahara

Relevant to you as a Moroccan — assume zero prior knowledge.

Western Sahara is a former Spanish colony that Morocco has administered since 1975, when Spain withdrew. The Polisario Front, backed mainly by Algeria, wants an independent Sahrawi state (the Sahrawi are the region's indigenous people) and has fought Morocco on and off since then.

A UN-monitored ceasefire has held since 1991, but a political settlement never followed. The two positions:

  • Morocco proposes autonomy under Moroccan sovereignty — the territory governs some of its own affairs, but remains part of Morocco.
  • Polisario and Algeria want a UN-run independence referendum — a direct vote in which Sahrawis choose independence or integration.

The diplomatic trend: the US recognised Moroccan sovereignty over the territory in 2020, and more countries (recently France, plus several African and Latin American states) have followed or endorsed the autonomy plan as "the only viable basis" for a settlement.

The counter-current: several states and EU institutions still do not formally recognise Moroccan sovereignty. The European Court of Justice ruled that Sahrawi people were not properly consulted before EU–Morocco trade deals covering the territory were signed — a legal opening that opponents of Morocco's position keep citing.

Morocco's most-read domestic outlets (Hespress, Morocco World News) cover this file almost daily and frame it consistently as a closed question of national sovereignty. Reading them alongside Al Jazeera or Algerian state media is a good way to see framing differences up close.

Sudan's Civil War

Assume zero prior knowledge.

Since April 2023, Sudan's national army (the SAF — Sudanese Armed Forces) has been fighting a paramilitary force called the RSF (Rapid Support Forces). A paramilitary is an armed group organised like an army but not the country's official military.

How two allies became enemies: the SAF and RSF jointly ran the country after a 2019 revolution ousted longtime dictator Omar al-Bashir. They were supposed to merge into one national force — but fell out over how and when that merger would happen, and who would command it. That dispute became open war.

Why it matters beyond Sudan:

  • It has produced the world's largest displacement crisis — more people forced from their homes than in any other current conflict.
  • Outside powers have backed both sides, turning a domestic power struggle into a regional proxy contest.
  • Both sides now use drones heavily. A Yale researcher described this as "the future of warfare in Africa" — a pattern other conflicts on the continent may follow.

It is badly under-covered internationally relative to its scale, which is part of why it's worth deliberately following.

Mali & the Sahel's Shift Away from France

Assume zero prior knowledge.

The Sahel is the dry belt of land just south of the Sahara desert, stretching across Africa — Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger are the key countries here. Since roughly 2012 the region has faced jihadist insurgencies: armed extremist groups (some linked to al-Qaeda or ISIS) fighting national governments and seizing rural territory.

The big shift: Mali, Burkina Faso, and Niger have each had military coups since 2020 (a coup = the army seizes power by force). The new military governments:

  1. Expelled French troops, who had been fighting the insurgents since 2013 (France is the former colonial power in this region).
  2. Brought in Russian forces instead — the Wagner Group, a Russian private military company, now rebranded as "Africa Corps."

Has it worked? Not on security: insurgent and ambush attacks remain frequent — for example, a convoy of Malian troops and Russian fighters was ambushed with dozens killed. But the political realignment away from France and toward Russia has reshaped the region's alliances, and is a live example of great-power competition (US / France / Russia / China) playing out on African soil.

Ukraine & Russia

Assume zero prior knowledge. This is the great-power conflict everything else orbits.

Ukraine is a large European country that was part of the Soviet Union until it broke up in 1991. Since then Ukraine has drifted westward — toward the EU and NATO (the US-led Western military alliance) — which Russia sees as a threat on its own border.

How we got to war: in 2014 Russia seized Crimea (a Ukrainian peninsula) and backed separatists in eastern Ukraine. Then in February 2022, Russia launched a full-scale invasion aiming to topple Ukraine's government. It failed to take the capital; the war settled into a grinding fight in the east and south.

Why it connects to everything you follow: - Energy — Russia is a top oil and gas exporter. The war reshaped Europe's energy map and pushed prices up worldwide (see the Energy and Oil thread). - The Sahel — the same Russian mercenary force fighting in Ukraine, Wagner / Africa Corps, operates in Mali and Niger. One actor, two continents (see Wagner Africa Corps). - Great-power rivalry — it's the most direct US/Europe vs. Russia contest, with China watching closely (see Great Power Rivalry). - Food — Ukraine is a huge grain exporter; the war raised bread prices across Africa and the Middle East.

The lens: this is a Great Power Rivalry story first, a European story second. Watch energy and weapons flows to read it.

Yemen & the Houthis

Assume zero prior knowledge. The clearest example of how the Iran axis and global shipping connect.

Yemen is a poor country at the southern tip of the Arabian Peninsula, next to Saudi Arabia and beside one of the world's busiest shipping lanes.

The war: in 2014 a rebel movement called the Houthis — from Yemen's Zaidi Shia minority — seized the capital, Sanaa. In 2015 Saudi Arabia led a coalition (backed by the West) to push them back. Years of war created one of the world's worst humanitarian crises. The Houthis now control much of the country.

Why they matter far beyond Yemen: - The Iran axis — the Houthis are part of Iran's Axis of Resistance. When Gaza flares, the Houthis open a front in solidarity. - The Red Sea — they sit beside the Bab el-Mandeb strait. Since 2023 they've attacked commercial ships, forcing much of global trade to reroute around Africa — raising shipping costs worldwide (the Energy and Oil thread).

The connection to see: a single decision in Gaza or Tehran can show up as a more expensive container ship in the Red Sea, which shows up as higher prices in a shop in Morocco. That chain — proxy → chokepoint → your wallet — is the whole point of the Connections tab.

The lens: read Yemen through Proxy Warfare and Energy and Oil, not as an isolated civil war.

Syria

Assume zero prior knowledge. A case study in how one civil war pulls in every outside power.

Syria sits at the crossroads of the Middle East. For decades it was ruled by the Assad family, a dictatorship.

The war: in 2011, peaceful protests (part of the "Arab Spring" uprisings) were crushed, and Syria spiralled into a brutal civil war. It fractured into zones held by the government, rebel groups, Kurdish forces, and jihadists (ISIS controlled a large area for years). Outside powers poured in: - Russia and Iran propped up Assad. - The US backed Kurdish-led forces against ISIS. - Turkey intervened against Kurdish groups. - Israel struck Iranian targets inside Syria.

It became the textbook case of a local war turned into an everyone's proxy battlefield (see Proxy Warfare).

The turn: in late 2024, a sudden rebel offensive toppled the Assad government — a huge shock that reshuffled the whole board and dealt a blow to both Russia and Iran, who lost a key ally.

Why it connects: - Refugees — over a decade of displacement reshaped Lebanon, Turkey, Jordan and European politics (the Displacement and Refugees thread). - The Iran axis — Syria was the land bridge linking Iran to Hezbollah in Lebanon.

The lens: Syria shows how Russia, Iran, and the US all use one country as a chessboard.

DR Congo & the Great Lakes

Assume zero prior knowledge. The conflict that connects war directly to your phone and to the Science tab.

The Democratic Republic of the Congo (DR Congo, or DRC) is a vast country in central Africa — enormously rich in minerals, yet one of the poorest places on earth. Its mineral-rich east has been unstable for nearly 30 years.

The conflict: the aftermath of the 1994 Rwandan genocide spilled into eastern Congo, and it has never fully stabilised since. Dozens of armed groups operate there. The most prominent recent one, M23, is widely reported to be backed by neighbouring Rwanda — which DRC and the UN accuse of using the group to control mineral-rich territory. In early 2025 M23 seized major cities including Goma.

Why it's the key "connector" conflict: - Minerals → technology — eastern Congo holds much of the world's cobalt and coltan, essential for the batteries and chips in phones, electric cars, and the AI hardware you read about under Science & Tech. The war is partly a fight over these (the Resources and Minerals thread). - The clean-tech irony — the "green" and "AI" boom increases demand for these minerals, raising the stakes of the conflict.

The connection to see: the Science tab and the Africa tab are reading about the same supply chain from opposite ends. That's the clearest proof that "separate" sections are really one story.

The lens: follow the minerals (see the Analyst's Toolkit, "Follow the ______").

2023 — Oct 7: Hamas attacks southern Israel, killing ~1,200 and taking ~250 hostages. Israel launches its largest-ever Gaza offensive.

2024 — The war grinds on: mass displacement, famine warnings, a soaring death toll, and international genocide accusations that Israel rejects.

2025 — October: A US-brokered ceasefire takes effect, built around a "Yellow Line" marking an Israeli withdrawal.

Timeline — Western Sahara

1973 — The Polisario Front is founded to fight for Sahrawi independence from Spain.

1975 — - The International Court of Justice finds historic ties but no basis to deny self-determination. - Morocco's "Green March" sends ~350,000 civilians into the territory. - The Madrid Accords: Spain withdraws and hands administration to Morocco and Mauritania.

1976 — Polisario declares the Sahrawi Arab Democratic Republic (SADR). War begins. Tens of thousands of Sahrawis flee to refugee camps near Tindouf, Algeria — where many still live.

1979 — Mauritania gives up its claim; Morocco takes over the whole territory.

1980s — Morocco builds a 2,700 km sand berm (defensive wall) that still splits the territory today.

1991 — A UN-monitored ceasefire ends the war; the UN mission MINURSO is created to organise an independence referendum — which never happens, stuck on who is eligible to vote.

2007 — Morocco proposes its autonomy plan: self-rule under Moroccan sovereignty. Polisario insists on a referendum with independence on the ballot.

2010 — The Gdeim Izik protest camp near Laâyoune is broken up, with deaths on both sides.

2020 — - A flare-up at the Guerguerat border crossing; Polisario declares the ceasefire void. - The US recognises Moroccan sovereignty over Western Sahara (tied to Morocco normalising ties with Israel).

2021 — Low-level fighting resumes along the berm after nearly 30 years of calm.

2022 — Spain shifts to back Morocco's autonomy plan, straining Spain–Algeria relations.

2024 — France formally endorses the autonomy plan as the basis for a settlement — a major diplomatic win for Rabat.

2026 — Momentum keeps tilting toward Morocco: more African, Arab and Latin American states back autonomy, and US lawmakers visit Dakhla. The counter-current stays legal — an EU court ruling on Sahrawi consent — while Algeria and Polisario still demand a referendum.

Glossary

A growing list. Add your own terms by editing content/glossary.md.

Conflict & diplomacy

Ceasefire — a formal agreement to stop fighting, usually temporary. It is not a peace treaty; fighting often resumes.

Blockade — sealing off a place so goods and people can't move freely in or out. Can be by land, sea, or air.

Occupation — one country controlling territory it does not consider part of its own recognised state, usually militarily (e.g. Israel in the West Bank).

Proxy war — a conflict where major powers back opposing local forces instead of fighting each other directly. Iran's "Axis of Resistance" groups are proxies.

Sanctions — economic penalties (trade bans, asset freezes) one country imposes on another to pressure it without war.

Sovereignty — a state's recognised authority to govern a territory. The core of the Western Sahara dispute.

Autonomy plan — Morocco's proposal to let Western Sahara govern local affairs while remaining under Moroccan sovereignty.

Referendum — a direct public vote on a single question (e.g. independence yes/no).

Annexation — one state formally absorbing territory into itself.

Institutions

UN Security Council resolution — a formal decision by the UN's most powerful body (15 members, 5 with veto power). Binding in principle, but enforcement depends on member states.

European Court of Justice (ECJ) — the EU's top court; its rulings bind EU institutions.

International Court of Justice (ICJ) — the UN's court for disputes between states.

Actors & terms

Axis of Resistance — the network of Iran-aligned armed groups: Hezbollah (Lebanon), the Houthis (Yemen), Hamas (Gaza), and Iraqi militias.

Paramilitary — an armed group organised like an army but outside the official military (e.g. Sudan's RSF).

Jihadist insurgency — an armed rebellion by extremist groups claiming a religious justification (as in the Sahel).

Uranium enrichment — processing uranium to higher concentrations; low levels fuel power plants, very high levels can fuel a bomb. The heart of the Iran nuclear dispute.

Strait of Hormuz — a narrow sea passage carrying ~20% of the world's oil shipments; closing it spikes global oil prices.

Nakba — Arabic for "catastrophe"; the 1948 displacement of ~700,000 Palestinians.

Thread — Great-Power Rivalry

The pattern: the US, China, and Russia compete for influence everywhere, usually through local conflicts rather than by fighting each other directly. Most "local" wars are also a venue for this bigger contest.

Where it shows up: - Ukraine — the most direct US/Europe vs. Russia contest. - The Sahel (Mali, Niger, Burkina Faso) — juntas swap French/US troops for Russian ones. - The Iran war — the US backs Israel and the Gulf; Russia and China tilt toward Iran. - Africa broadly — China builds infrastructure and buys minerals; the US and Russia counter. - Western Sahara — even this is shaped by which powers back Rabat vs. Algiers.

Watch for: when a small country suddenly changes partners (expels one power, invites another), that's this thread moving. Ask what each great power gets from the switch.

Connects to: Energy and Oil, Proxy Warfare, Resources and Minerals.

Thread — Energy & Oil

The pattern: whoever controls the production and shipping of oil and gas holds a lever over the whole world economy. A conflict near an energy chokepoint or a big producer instantly becomes everyone's problem — which is why markets react to wars far away.

Where it shows up: - The Strait of Hormuz (Iran war) — ~20% of the world's oil passes through it; closing it spikes prices globally. - The Red Sea / Houthis (Yemen) — attacks on tankers reroute global shipping. - Ukraine–Russia — Russia is a top gas/oil exporter; the war reshaped Europe's energy map. - The Gulf states — their wealth and diplomacy run on hydrocarbons.

Watch for: a price move in oil is a "tell." If crude jumps, scan for what happened near a producer or a chokepoint — the political story is usually upstream of the market story.

Why it matters to you: Morocco imports most of its energy, so these spikes hit Moroccan fuel and food prices directly.

Connects to: Great Power Rivalry, Displacement and Refugees.

Thread — Proxy Warfare

What it means: a proxy is a local armed group that a bigger power funds, arms, and directs so it can fight a rival without sending its own army (cheaper, deniable, less risky at home).

The pattern: many "civil wars" are partly proxy contests. To understand them, look past the local fighters to their sponsors.

Where it shows up: - Iran's "Axis of Resistance" — Hezbollah (Lebanon), Hamas (Gaza), the Houthis (Yemen), Iraqi militias. One sponsor, many fronts. - Russia's Wagner / Africa Corps — Russia's proxy force across the Sahel and Syria. - Sudan — the SAF and RSF each draw on outside backers. - The Gulf — rival states have backed opposing sides in Yemen and Libya.

Watch for: when the same weapons or tactics appear in far-apart conflicts, suspect a shared sponsor. Ask "whose proxy is this, and what does the sponsor want?" — the local group's stated cause is only half the story.

Connects to: Great Power Rivalry, Religion and Sect.

Thread — Colonial Legacy

The pattern: many of today's borders, grievances, and alliances were set by European empires (Britain, France, Spain, Ottoman) — often drawing lines that ignored who actually lived there. Decades later, those lines are still what people fight over.

Where it shows up: - Israel / Palestine — the dispute traces to the end of the British Mandate in 1948. - Western Sahara — a former Spanish colony whose status was never resolved when Spain left in 1975. - The Sahel — France's former colonies, now expelling French troops and turning to Russia (a direct reaction against the colonial relationship). - Sudan / South Sudan — British-era divisions still shape the conflict.

Watch for: when a country dramatically breaks with a former colonial power, that's this thread. The move is often as much about identity and sovereignty as about practical security.

Why it matters to you: Morocco and the whole Maghreb sit inside this thread — colonial borders, the France relationship, and the Sahara file all belong here.

Connects to: Great Power Rivalry, Proxy Warfare.

Thread — Resources & Minerals

The pattern: control of valuable resources — oil, gold, cobalt, lithium, rare earths, water, farmland — funds wars and attracts outside powers. Follow the resource map and you can often predict where conflict appears.

Where it shows up: - DR Congo — cobalt and coltan, essential for batteries and electronics, help fund armed groups in the east. - Sudan — gold revenue bankrolls both sides of the war. - The Sahel — gold and uranium (Niger) draw in outside partners. - The Gulf & Iran — oil, again.

The bridge to your Science tab: the cobalt and lithium fought over in Congo end up in the batteries and AI chips you read about under Science & Tech. The "clean tech" supply chain starts in conflict zones. Politics and technology are the same story here.

Watch for: a spike in demand for a mineral (e.g. an AI or EV boom) tends to raise the stakes — and the violence — wherever that mineral is mined.

Connects to: Great Power Rivalry, Energy and Oil.

Thread — Displacement & Refugees

The pattern: war forces people from their homes. Where they flee to becomes part of the story — pressuring neighbouring countries, reshaping their politics, and sometimes spreading the conflict. Displacement is both a consequence and a cause.

Where it shows up: - Sudan — the world's largest displacement crisis; millions pushed into Chad, Egypt, and beyond. - Gaza — mass internal displacement within a sealed territory. - Syria — over a decade of refugees reshaped Lebanon, Jordan, Turkey, and European politics. - The Sahel — insurgencies empty rural areas and swell fragile cities. - Western Sahara — Sahrawi refugees have lived in camps near Tindouf, Algeria, since the 1970s.

Watch for: refugee flows are a leading indicator. A surge tells you a conflict is intensifying before the headline numbers catch up — and tells you which neighbouring country gets pulled in next.

Why it matters to you: migration across the Mediterranean and the Sahara is a core Morocco/Europe issue, sitting right on this thread.

Connects to: Colonial Legacy, Religion and Sect.

Thread — Religion & Sect

A careful note: religion is often used to mobilise people, but it's rarely the whole cause. Power, land, and money usually sit underneath. Treat "religious war" as a starting question, not an answer — ask what material interest the religious language is carrying.

The pattern: sectarian identity (especially the Sunni–Shia split in Islam) is a fault line that powers align along and exploit.

Where it shows up: - The Iran axis — Shia-led Iran backs Shia and allied groups (Hezbollah, the Houthis) against Sunni-led rivals like Saudi Arabia. But it's as much about power as theology — Iran allies with Sunni Hamas too. - The Sahel & Nigeria — jihadist insurgencies use religious framing over local grievances. - Sudan, Syria, Iraq — sect maps onto political power struggles.

Watch for: when leaders reach for religious language, ask what practical thing it's mobilising people toward. Follow that, not just the label.

The Analyst's Toolkit

How to read any political story — even one about a place you've never heard of. Run these lenses in order. With practice they become automatic.

1 · The five questions

Ask them of every story, in this order:

  1. Who are the actors? Which states, groups, institutions, or leaders are in the room? (Check the Actors tab — most stories reuse the same ~15 players.)
  2. What does each one want? Not what they say — what outcome benefits them. Territory, security, legitimacy, resources, staying in power.
  3. Who benefits from this event? If a pipeline blows up or a ceasefire collapses, ask cui bono — "who gains?" It's often not who you'd guess.
  4. What's the framing? The same fact is "liberation" to one outlet and "aggression" to another. (The Perspectives tab is built for exactly this.)
  5. What's the root, and what's the ripple? Every story has a history (check the Timeline) and a second-order effect (check the Threads).

2 · Follow the ______

A shortcut for question 3. When a story is confusing, follow one of these and it usually clarifies:

  • Follow the oil / gas. Who ships energy through here? A price spike explains a lot of "sudden" moves.
  • Follow the weapons. Who armed whom? Proxy wars are visible in the supply chain.
  • Follow the refugees. Displacement reveals who's losing, and pressures neighbouring countries into the story.
  • Follow the minerals. Cobalt, lithium, rare earths — resource maps predict where "surprise" conflicts appear.
  • Follow the vote / the money. Domestic elections and budgets often drive "foreign policy."

3 · The connection habit

After you understand a story on its own, connect it:

  • Which other conflict shares an actor with this one? (Russia is in Ukraine and Mali. Iran is in Gaza and Yemen.)
  • Which thread is this an example of? (Is this really an energy story? A great-power rivalry story wearing a local costume?)

Do this three times and the "separate" conflicts start to look like one connected board — which is what they are.

4 · Guard against your own shortcuts

  • Beware the single source. One outlet is a hypothesis, not a fact. Confirm across the Perspectives set.
  • Beware the tidy story. Real politics is messy; a narrative that's too clean is usually missing something.
  • Beware your team. Notice when you're relieved a fact is true because it flatters "your side." That relief is the bias to check.
  • Numbers vary by source and date — especially casualty figures. Compare, don't adopt the first one you see.
